Investigate what failed before changing the system

Investigate four fictional incidents step by step: cache storms, duplicate payments, stranded fulfillment and mixed-version releases.

Technical definitionA failure investigation reconstructs the observed business outcome from durable state and telemetry, tests competing hypotheses, and distinguishes immediate mitigation from permanent repair.
Before you begin. Read evidence and identity and unknown outcomes. These are fictional teaching cases, not reports of actual incidents.

Learning outcome: Build a timeline, select safe mitigations and define evidence of recovery.

1. Use a repeatable investigation method

Start with customer impact and the invariant at risk. Preserve a time window, affected population and identities before logs rotate. Form at least two plausible explanations; an alarming graph alone is not causation. Compare changes, dependency behavior and business state. Mitigate using the smallest action that protects correctness, then verify recovery through completed business outcomes.

Use this sequence in each case: symptom → evidence → competing hypotheses → discriminating check → mitigation → repair → recovery proof. Do not replay work merely because a queue is quiet or an HTTP call timed out. First establish whether the effect already happened.

2. Case 1: a cache expiry becomes a database incident

Symptom: catalog p99 rises after a popular collection expires. Application CPU is moderate; database connections and lease wait spike. Hypotheses: a query regression, database resource loss, or synchronized misses. Correlate expiry/miss rate, active queries, query plans and the deployment timeline. Stable plans plus a sudden concentration of identical reads support the stampede hypothesis.

Mitigation: constrain refill and fallback concurrency, serve permitted stale catalog data, and shed lower-priority work. Increasing every pod’s connection pool would add pressure. Repair: per-key request coalescing, TTL jitter where appropriate, bounded refresh and admission control. Local single-flight reduces duplication within one process; fleet-wide behavior still needs analysis.

Recovery proof: source query load returns within its budget, oldest waits shrink, cache freshness stays within its contract, and checkout success does not degrade. A recovered hit ratio alone is insufficient. See the cache race explanation.

3. Case 2: a customer reports two charges

Symptom: two provider charges map to one intended checkout. Hypotheses: duplicated HTTP attempts, webhook replay, or reconciliation creating a fresh provider operation. Compare tenant, order ID, local operation key, request fingerprint and provider operation IDs across attempts. Trace IDs can differ during a legitimate retry and cannot establish business uniqueness.

Discriminating check: did an earlier call time out after provider success, followed by a call with a new key? If so, local uniqueness may be intact while the remote boundary is unprotected. Mitigation: stop unsafe automatic reissue for affected states, reconcile provider records and handle refunds under the established business policy. Do not bulk-mark unknown payments failed.

Repair: stable provider identity, durable UNKNOWN state, bounded reconciliation and explicit retention behavior. Recovery proof: every affected local operation maps to its known provider outcome, unresolved cases have owners, and crash-window tests no longer produce a second effect.

4. Case 3: a healthy broker hides a stalled business flow

Symptom: paid orders remain unfulfilled while broker delivery is healthy. Hypotheses: publication failed, consumers lost capacity, a poison schema repeats, or remote fulfillment is slow. Compare oldest business-state age, outbox lag, delivery rate, consumer commit rate and quarantine records. Queue depth alone may fall when messages move to a dead-letter queue.

Mitigation: isolate the incompatible population, retain event identity and restore a compatible reader. Replaying without a durable consumer guard can create duplicate shipments. Repair: compatibility checks, per-event failure isolation and an explicit replay protocol. Decide whether preserving order for an aggregate requires pausing later events rather than skipping the bad one.

Recovery proof: affected paid orders reach the correct terminal fulfillment state, oldest age drains and replay does not duplicate shipment identity. See acknowledgement boundaries.

5. Case 4: a successful rollout causes intermittent errors

Version B writes a new status that version A cannot parse. Probes pass because they never read that state. Errors appear only on A pods and only for recently changed orders. Distinguish this from network flakiness by joining version, state and failure class with bounded telemetry dimensions.

Disable the incompatible writer feature if that is safe, retain evidence and deploy a tolerant reader. Rolling all pods back to A can spread the failure because new data survives. Prevent recurrence using expand/contract sequencing, mixed-version contract tests and backward-compatible readers before writers. Recovery requires checking the already-written population, not just the next health probe.

6. Incident interview follow-ups

IntermediateWhy not restart everything first?
It can destroy evidence, multiply cold-start load and replay work without knowing previous outcomes. Protect the invariant and collect minimal decisive evidence; restart when there is a supported hypothesis that it helps.
AdvancedHow do you prove the mitigation worked?
Observe restored customer outcomes, bounded backlog age and correctness of affected records over an explicit interval. Resource graphs alone may recover while business work stays stranded.
