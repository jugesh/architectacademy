Architecture Decision Guides · fundamentals to production judgment

Choose a mechanism by the guarantee it must supply

Start with an invariant, compare the mechanisms that can enforce it, and write a decision you can defend through a crash.

Technical definitionAn architecture decision connects a requirement to a mechanism, states the rejected alternatives, and identifies the evidence that would justify revisiting it.
Before you begin. Read database correctness, message boundaries and consistency.

Learning outcome: Write a decision that survives a concrete failure scenario, rather than selecting a product by familiarity.

1. Start with one decision, not the whole stack

The checkout service must reserve stock before confirming an order. “Use PostgreSQL and Kafka” is not yet a decision: neither name explains which operation prevents overselling. Write the rule first: for a given SKU, available stock must remain nonnegative and a repeated reservation command must not allocate stock again. Then locate the authority that can enforce the rule under concurrent requests. A local conditional update may be sufficient; a distributed workflow becomes relevant only when ownership crosses a commit boundary.

A useful decision record contains context, required guarantees, options, chosen approach, failure behavior, operational cost and revisit triggers. Record what was measured and what remains an assumption. An assumption such as “one database can serve the next year” needs an explicit load-test or utilization trigger, not an indefinitely valid promise.

2. Separate hard rules from preferences

InputExampleEffect on choice
InvariantNo negative stockNeeds atomic enforcement at the stock authority.
FreshnessBrowse availability may lag five secondsPermits a projection for browsing, not final reservation.
TargetIllustrative p95 reservation under 150 msConstrains coordination and critical-path dependencies.
ConstraintTeam operates PostgreSQL and EKSMakes an unfamiliar store an additional cost requiring evidence.

Preferences can be traded. An invariant cannot be silently weakened to reach a latency target. If requirements conflict, expose the conflict to product owners through concrete outcomes: “the page may show a seat that another buyer has just claimed” differs from “two buyers receive confirmed ownership.”

3. SQL or NoSQL: derive from operations

Choose whenCandidateProve before adoption

Do not compare product categories in the abstract. Express a real operation, expected key distribution and transaction scope. A single hot inventory key remains a coordination point in any store. Splitting that key requires a business allocation protocol, not merely a new database brand.

4. REST or messaging: start with completion semantics

Use a synchronous request when the caller needs a bounded immediate answer and the dependency fits the deadline. Use a durable asynchronous handoff when acceptance can be separated from completion. The API must then expose pending, terminal and recovery states. A message bus does not make the caller’s original operation complete.

QuestionSynchronous choiceAsynchronous choice
Must the caller learn a final result now?Return the result within a bounded deadline.Return acceptance plus a durable operation ID when deferral is allowed.
What survives a process crash?Durable local operation state and retry identity.Durable handoff plus deduplication and replay policy.
What does coupling cost?Availability and latency of required dependencies.Schema evolution, backlog, eventual completion and recovery ownership.

Spring HTTP clients, Kafka clients and SQS SDKs implement communication. Application state machines still own business completion. Retry libraries cannot decide whether another charge is safe.

5. Transaction, saga, or reservation?

Prefer a local transaction when the relevant facts share one authority and commit boundary. When independent authorities are necessary, describe the intermediate states first. A saga coordinates local commits and recovery; a reservation temporarily allocates a resource while later steps run. A saga may use reservations, and neither makes remote effects part of a local ACID transaction.

For booking, reserve a seat until a defined expiry. If payment completes after expiry, a coordinator must apply a declared rule: obtain a new reservation if allowed, otherwise refund or route to manual recovery. A queue, retry annotation or distributed lock cannot decide that business policy.

6. Worked decision record: protect inventory

Context: one inventory authority; requests can repeat; payment is external. Decision: reserve using a positive-quantity conditional SQL update and insert the reservation and outbox in one transaction. Scope a unique operation ID by tenant. Keep payment outside the lock-holding transaction and persist its unknown/terminal states.

Rejected: Java read-then-write, because concurrent buyers can both pass; a Redis-only lock, because ownership can expire while work continues and it does not itself make SQL mutations atomic; moving inventory to another store without a measured need, because it adds migration work without removing the invariant.

Trade-offs: hot SKUs serialize, reservations need expiry and reconciliation, and payment completion can race cancellation. Evidence: race tests, affected-row checks, expiry/payment reconciliation and a contention benchmark. Revisit: measured hot-key throughput exceeds safe capacity or independent domain ownership becomes necessary.

7. Decision 1: cache the view, protect the purchase

The shop’s product description is read frequently, while merchant changes are occasional. Browse responses may lag under a stated policy; checkout must apply current pricing and stock rules. This is the trigger for a cache decision: repeated read cost is measurable, and the product allows some copies to be stale. If the indexed query is already cheap at the projected workload, adding another dependency may not yet be justified.

First define the reusable representation: tenant, product, language, currency and any relevant personalization. Record the actual source-read cost and popularity distribution. Then compare options using the same failure conditions. Do not compare a warm-cache benchmark against an unindexed database scan and call the difference an architectural result.

AlternativeWhat it providesFailure/cost to own
Indexed source onlyOne authority and simpler freshnessRepeated source work; bound capacity and query cost.
Local Caffeine viewNo network hop on local hitsIndependent copies; cold starts and cross-instance freshness.
Shared Redis/Valkey viewReusable shared representationNetwork/serialization dependency; miss/refill races and source fallback.
Public HTTP/CDN cacheReuse near public readersCache-key/privacy rules; personalized representations require a different policy.

Selected under these assumptions: use a shared product-description view only where measured savings justify it, and edge-cache public media. Keep personalized discounts separate. Checkout uses authoritative rules rather than trusting the browse copy. Commit source updates with durable invalidation intent if lost invalidation needs recovery. The reader/writer refill race still needs the selected freshness protocol; the outbox does not make cache publication part of the source transaction.

Rejected shortcut: put stock in a cache and rely on TTL for correctness. Expiry does not coordinate two purchases. Likewise, catching every cache exception and falling through to SQL can turn cache loss into database overload. Cap fallback/refill, use permitted stale data or reject optional reads, and measure the protected checkout outcome.

Evidence gate: compare source work and useful completion latency under warm/cold cache, a hot key, slow old fills, invalidation delay and tenant-key variation. Revisit: freshness needs tighten, personalization changes the key space, hit distribution changes or the miss path cannot meet the failure contract. The decision record should retain those assumptions and measurements, not just the selected product name.

8. Decision 2: move receipt delivery off the request

Checkout is durable after its local transaction, but email latency holds the customer connection. Product accepts eventual receipt delivery and requires the intent not to vanish after acceptance. That permits separating order acceptance from receipt completion. It does not permit calling an in-memory task queue a durable delivery contract.

AlternativeAcceptance and recoveryReason to choose/reject
Synchronous provider callCaller waits; response can be uncertainChoose only if bounded completion must be part of this contract and recovery is explicit.
In-memory async executorFast handoff within process lifetimeInsufficient as the only record of required accepted work.
Local outbox plus workerOrder and intent commit together; relay may repeatFits durable deferred work when consumer/provider repetition is handled.
Retained event log with independent consumersReplay and separate progress by groupUseful when those requirements justify partition, retention and consumer operations.

Selected under these assumptions: commit OrderAccepted/receipt intent with the local order state, relay it to the chosen queue and create a repeat-safe local delivery task. Keep notification identity separate from delivery attempts. Choose SQS-style work dispatch when one durable job owner is enough; choose a Kafka-style retained log when independent replay/order requirements and operations justify it. Both require effect-specific recovery.

The provider can accept the email while its response disappears. If it supports stable identity or inquiry, use that contract; otherwise state the residual duplicate-delivery risk and the policy for ambiguous attempts. An inbox proves only the protected local task/mutation boundary. It cannot make a human receive exactly one message under every provider failure.

Evidence gate: interrupt after order commit, after publish, after local task commit and after provider acceptance. Preserve identity and prove the intent remains recoverable without issuing uncontrolled repeats. Measure accepted-to-delivered age and unresolved attempts, not only queue depth. Revisit: receipt completion becomes mandatory before order confirmation, replay requirements change, the provider contract changes or backlog cannot meet the target.

9. Decision 3: retain the relational stock authority

Two buyers compete for the last unit. The service already owns inventory and reservations in one relational database. The rule is that available stock never becomes negative and one intended reservation is not applied twice. Begin with the atomic decision and workload, not a general claim that one database category scales better.

AlternativeInvariant boundaryTrade-off
Conditional SQL mutation plus reservation transactionStock check/decrement and local reservation commit togetherHot rows, transaction duration, deadlocks and pool capacity require measurement.
Redis lock around SQL writesSeparate temporary ownership and durable stateLease expiry/stale owners and cross-system failure gaps; lock alone is not the invariant.
DynamoDB conditional/transactional modelAppropriate DynamoDB-owned items under its exact protocolMigration, access model, key skew and transaction/read-path semantics.
Partitioned stock allocationsSeparate bounded ownership portionsChanges allocation/rebalancing and product availability behavior; not merely more shards.

Selected under these assumptions: validate positive quantity, enforce stable scoped command identity and perform the conditional decrement with reservation/outbox changes in one transaction. Check affected rows, update multi-item operations in a deliberate order and handle bounded whole-transaction retry where required. Keep the payment provider outside lock-holding work and preserve unknown outcome recovery.

A Redis lock is rejected as the only correctness mechanism because a paused worker can return after lease expiry, and the lock does not atomically commit the SQL effect. A store migration is rejected without evidence that the current authority cannot meet the workload or ownership need. That does not declare another store inferior; it identifies an unproven benefit and a definite new migration boundary.

Evidence gate: concurrent last-item requests, duplicate identities, rollback after reservation failure, deadlock retry, expiry/payment races and representative hot-SKU contention. Revisit: measured sustainable throughput or team/data ownership changes. Any replacement must preserve the same invariant and failure tests, with a controlled write-ownership cutover.

10. Defend the decision

IntermediateWhy not choose the fastest database?
A benchmark without the actual invariant, key distribution and failure mode can measure an irrelevant operation. Establish required semantics first, then compare sustainable throughput and operational cost.
AdvancedWhen is a distributed lock justified?
When exclusive ownership is necessary and a defined lease/fencing protocol can prevent obsolete owners from acting. Prefer authoritative conditional mutations when they already enforce the invariant; a lock alone is not durable effect deduplication.
IntermediateWhy compare the same failure scenario for every alternative?
Warm happy-path performance can hide the cost or correctness weakness of one option. Hold workload, invariants, freshness and failure assumptions constant, then compare useful completion, recovery and operating burden.
AdvancedWhat should a rejected alternative record contain?
The requirement it fails or the cost not justified by current evidence, its remaining merits, and the assumption change that would warrant reconsideration. Avoid permanent product rankings unsupported by the actual operation.

11. Read with a question

  • PostgreSQL isolation: check which anomalies a proposed protocol permits.
  • Saga orchestration: inspect distributed recovery rather than assuming rollback.
