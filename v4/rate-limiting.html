Rate Limiting & Backpressure · fundamentals to production judgment

Admit only the work the system can finish usefully

Protect checkout from noisy clients and slow dependencies by distinguishing request rate, concurrency, queues, quotas and downstream demand.

Technical definitionRate limiting bounds admitted operations over time. Backpressure controls production/admission in response to consumption capacity. A concurrency limit bounds simultaneous work; a quota bounds consumption over a longer accounting period.
Before you begin. Read throughput and concurrency and deadlines. We begin with a finite worker pool, not a limiter algorithm.

Learning outcome: Choose a fair admission policy, implement atomic accounting and design controlled behavior during limiter failure.

1. A fast caller can consume a slow service

Scalability showed that finite dependencies can be overwhelmed before new capacity arrives. Rate limiting decides which arrivals may enter under a policy; backpressure/admission control align accepted work with processing capacity. Protecting a service therefore involves both usage allocation and overload behavior.

Keep rate, quota and concurrency separate. A rate bounds arrivals over time, a quota bounds accumulated use over an allocation period, and concurrency bounds active work. Fifty requests per second can be safe at 100 ms and exhausting at two seconds. A fixed rate policy alone does not bound the latter wait population.

2. Distinguish arrivals from work already in progress

Read this slowly: it establishes the distinction used in the implementation sections.

A rate limit bounds arrivals over time. A quota bounds total usage over a longer allocation period. A concurrency limit bounds active work. If requests become slower, an unchanged arrival rate can still produce too much simultaneous work. This is why a request-per-second rule is only one part of overload control.

Suppose 50 requests arrive each second and complete in 100 ms: average in-flight work is about five. A dependency slowdown to two seconds raises that figure toward 100 under steady admitted traffic. A concurrency gate can reject or defer work before the scarce pool is exhausted. A bounded queue absorbs a short burst but cannot sustainably absorb arrivals above service capacity.

Check your understanding
FoundationalA queue solves overload without rejecting callers.
Only for bursts or work whose demand can eventually be served within its deadline. Sustained excess arrivals grow backlog, delay and cost. Bound queue capacity or age and define admission, expiry and recovery policies.
IntermediateHow do you protect small tenants from a large tenant?
Combine tenant-aware allocation with global capacity limits and bounded concurrent work. A global counter alone can be monopolized; independent per-tenant limits alone can exceed total capacity.

3. Define fairness and overload semantics

Define who shares capacity: user, tenant, credential, endpoint or service. A global limit can be monopolized by one tenant; independent tenant limits can collectively exceed total capacity. Combine allocation with a global resource budget and decide how bursts and priorities are handled.

State the response and recovery contract. Rejected work must not secretly commit a business effect. Deferred work needs durable acceptance and an age/deadline policy. A Retry-After hint should be compatible with the client’s overall deadline; it is not permission for synchronized unlimited retries.

Functional requirements

  • Apply per-partner and global admission limits.
  • Protect payment-call concurrency and queue size.
  • Tell rejected clients how to recover.

Targets and invariants

  • One tenant must not starve others.
  • No unbounded queues or retries.
  • Distributed limit precision is specified.
  • Limiter outage behavior is explicit per operation.

A business command rejected before acceptance should cause no side effect. Once accepted durably, responsibility changes: the service must complete or report its terminal failure through the stated protocol. Do not count a queued 202 as successful completion.

4. Choose an algorithm from burst behavior

A token bucket accumulates permits up to a capacity and replenishes them at a rate. It permits bounded bursts after idle time while constraining sustained admission. A fixed window is simple but can admit bursts at adjacent boundaries; a sliding approach smooths that behavior at additional state/coordination cost.

Choose the unit carefully. Requests with radically different cost should not always consume identical permits. Consider payload/query work and downstream resources. A concurrency semaphore protects active work, while a bounded queue absorbs some waiting. Specify acquisition timeout, release behavior and cancellation so an abandoned request does not leak a permit.

AlgorithmBehaviorTrade-off
Fixed windowCount requests in aligned periodsCan admit a double burst around a boundary.
Sliding log/windowAccount over a moving intervalMore storage/work or approximation.
Token bucketTokens refill; capacity permits a bounded burstRefill, capacity and cost units need definition.
Leaky bucketDrain at a controlled rateQueue delay/overflow policy matters.

A token bucket with capacity 100 and refill 10/s allows a saved burst of 100, then roughly 10/s over time. It does not guarantee a limit of ten requests in every individual second. Assign weighted cost if an export is more expensive than a tiny status read.

Atomic token-bucket decision (conceptual):
 elapsed = max(0, now - lastRefill)
 tokens = min(capacity, tokens + elapsed * refillRate)
 if tokens < cost: reject without mutation/effect
 else: tokens -= cost; admit
Persist balance + refill timestamp atomically.

Validate clocks and numeric bounds. Concurrent read-modify-write from Java without an atomic store operation can overspend tokens.

5. One limit across many replicas

With several application instances, independent local counters can admit several times a purported global limit. A shared atomic counter/script can coordinate a chosen scope but adds latency and an availability dependency. Hierarchical or approximate local budgets trade precision for reduced coordination; expose that trade-off instead of calling it an exact global guarantee.

Choose failure behavior by operation. Failing open can preserve availability while losing abuse/resource protection; failing closed can block legitimate work during a limiter outage. Keep sensitive invariants elsewhere at their authority. Clock, expiry, hot-key load and retry races need testing in the actual shared-state implementation.

6. REST, Spring and AWS mapping

At the edge, apply coarse body/rate controls to avoid spending application resources on obviously excessive traffic. In the application, enforce tenant/resource-aware admission and bound costly downstream concurrency. Gateway throttling and WAF rules do not automatically provide fair scheduling inside a shared worker or database pool.

Spring can integrate limiter and bulkhead libraries, and Redis can support selected shared algorithms, but configure the scope and failure policy explicitly. Measure attempted, admitted, rejected and completed work separately. Keep counters bounded and avoid a distinct metric time series for every unbounded identity.

Work the boundary through a failure

For a token bucket shared across instances, the authoritative operation must read the last refill time, compute available tokens, conditionally debit and persist the new state as one serialized mutation under the chosen store contract. Splitting a read and write across requests lets concurrent callers spend the same token. Use the authority’s time model consistently and bound elapsed-time arithmetic; clock behavior and idle-key expiry must not mint unexpected bursts.

Define the unavailable-store policy per operation. Allowing through protects availability but can breach a dependency budget; rejecting protects the budget but denies otherwise valid work. A bounded local fallback partitions or weakens the original guarantee, so explain its limit. Return an appropriate rejection signal and retry guidance, while still requiring clients to use backoff and a deadline.

A rate limit bounds admissions over time; it does not bound concurrent slow operations. Pair it with concurrency/queue limits when service time can grow, and measure rejects separately from admitted requests that later fail.

7. Worked design: noisy export partner

For a flash sale, reserve capacity for checkout and bound catalog refill separately. Give tenants a declared allocation and cap total database-facing work. When processing slows, the concurrency gate prevents indefinite accumulation even if arrival rate remains within its ordinary limit.

Test a burst at a window boundary, one noisy tenant, shared-limiter failure and a dependency slowdown. Verify rejected requests cause no business mutation, accepted durable work remains recoverable, and retry guidance does not amplify overload. Measure queue age and useful completions rather than celebrating a low rejection percentage alone.

8. Pitfalls and observability

An unbounded queue can make acceptance look successful while deadlines become impossible. Recalculate backlog drain using completion capacity minus new arrivals; if there is no positive margin, waiting will not resolve itself. Reject/defer according to the contract before accepting work without a viable completion path.

Fairness is a business and scheduling choice, not just a counter. Separate limits, priorities and queues can help, but starvation and bypass paths still need analysis. Protect administrative/recovery capacity so an incident does not prevent the operations required to repair it.

TrapFix
Return 429 after committing a side effectMake admission precede acceptance or return the accepted identity/outcome.

Monitor admitted/rejected work, concurrent operations, wait time, oldest job and limiter latency/errors. Confirm the limiter improves completed work and fairness rather than simply moving the queue into the client fleet.

9. Interview ladder

FoundationalRate versus concurrency limit?
Rate bounds arrivals over time; concurrency bounds simultaneous resource use. Slow calls can exhaust concurrency even under a modest rate.
FoundationalWhat does backpressure mean?
A mechanism that reduces or bounds upstream demand based on downstream capacity, instead of allowing indefinite accumulation.
IntermediateWhy can fixed windows burst at boundaries?
A client can spend one full allowance just before reset and another just after, concentrating two windows of traffic into a short interval.
IntermediateCan per-pod buckets enforce a strict global limit?
Not by themselves. Replicas multiply budgets unless the protocol partitions or coordinates a global allowance.
AdvancedFail open or fail closed when Redis fails?
Choose per operation from abuse/cost/correctness and availability requirements; use a bounded tested fallback if allowed, not an improvised universal rule.
AdvancedHow do you prevent lost permits after worker death?
Use bounded leases/reclamation with correct ownership/expiry and, where protected effects require it, fencing. Test paused workers as well as clean process termination.
IntermediateWhy can a service overload while requests stay below its rate limit?
Service time can increase, so the same arrival rate produces more simultaneous work. Bound active work and waiting at scarce dependencies separately from rate/quota allocation.
AdvancedHow do you combine tenant fairness with total capacity?
Allocate tenant-aware budgets while enforcing a global resource/concurrency budget. Independent local or per-tenant limits alone can collectively exceed capacity; a global limit alone can be monopolized. State precision and limiter-outage policies.
