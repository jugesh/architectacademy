11-DISTRIBUTED-SYSTEMS · TECHNICAL CHAPTER

Timeouts, Retries and Partial Failure

Why distributed calls fail ambiguously and how to avoid retry storms and cascading outages.

Partial failure is normal Deadline propagation Retry amplification Circuit breakers and bulkheads Load shedding and recovery

1. Partial failure is normal

What does a timeout prove?

In a single process, a function call either returns or throws in a relatively constrained environment. Across a network, the caller cannot reliably distinguish a failed server from a lost response or a partition. A timeout is a local decision to stop waiting. The remote operation may still be executing or already committed.

2. Deadline propagation

Why are independent 2s timeouts dangerous?

Allocate a request-level deadline and derive smaller per-hop budgets. If a 900 ms user-facing budget is spent 700 ms upstream, a downstream call should not start a fresh 2-second wait. Propagate cancellation where possible, but never assume cancellation rolls back remote side effects. Track remaining time, not just independent timeout constants.

3. Retry amplification

Who should own retries?

If three service layers each retry a failed call three times, the leaf dependency can see up to 27 attempts for one logical request, depending on interpretation of attempt count. Centralize retries at a deliberate boundary, use capped exponential backoff with jitter, and retry only errors likely to recover. Respect Retry-After and service-level budgets.

4. Circuit breakers and bulkheads

What does a breaker actually protect?

A circuit breaker temporarily stops calls to a failing dependency, preventing wasted work while allowing probes for recovery. It does not fix the dependency. Bulkheads bound concurrency or isolate pools so one slow integration cannot exhaust all request threads. A breaker without sensible fallback or load shedding can still produce a user-visible outage.

5. Load shedding and recovery

Why reject instead of queue forever?

When arrival rate exceeds sustainable service rate, queues grow and tail latency collapses. Bound queues, reject early with 429/503 where appropriate, and make clients back off. Recovery requires avoiding synchronized retry bursts and respecting downstream capacity. Use chaos tests for packet loss, slow responses, DNS errors and region failures.

Interview and design review

Try answering before expanding.
Why is a timeout not equivalent to a failure?

The client stopped waiting, but the server may still have committed the operation. Retrying without idempotency can duplicate effects.

How can retries trigger an outage?

Many clients retry together, amplifying demand on an already saturated dependency. Use jitter, caps, budgets and admission control.

When should you avoid retries?

For permanent validation errors, operations without a safe deduplication contract, or when the deadline/retry budget is exhausted.

What is the difference between bulkhead and circuit breaker?

A bulkhead limits resource consumption by an integration. A circuit breaker temporarily stops attempts based on observed failures. They address different failure propagation paths.

Retry-budget arithmetic

Why can retries delay recovery?

Assume each service layer allows 3 total attempts, including the first. Across three independently retrying layers, one original request may generate up to 3×3×3=27 leaf calls. If 500 original requests per second hit a degraded dependency, the worst-case attempt rate could reach 13,500 per second before backoff and other limits. This is why retries must be budgeted end to end. Prefer one intentional retry owner, cap attempts and elapsed time, apply full jitter, and honor the remaining request deadline. Couple retries with load shedding and an idempotency contract. Do not retry a timed-out payment POST unless the logical operation can be deduplicated.

Why can retries delay recovery?

Retries multiply demand precisely when a dependency has reduced capacity, increasing queueing and further timeouts.

Deadline and retry propagation

Trace the invariant, failure boundary and recovery.

A request has 800 ms total budget. After 250 ms in authentication and 150 ms in a database call, only 400 ms remain before accounting for serialization and network overhead. A downstream timeout of 1 second is therefore meaningless: it outlives the caller’s deadline. Propagate a deadline or remaining budget, reserve time for cleanup, and cancel work where possible. Retries must fit inside that budget and should be owned by one layer to avoid multiplicative amplification. If three layers each allow three attempts, one user request can produce up to 27 leaf attempts. Circuit breakers protect against repeated calls to a failing dependency, while bulkheads bound resource consumption; neither replaces admission control or an idempotency contract.

Design review and interview follow-ups

Should a timed-out POST be retried automatically?

Only if the operation has an appropriate idempotency or reconciliation contract and the retry fits within the remaining deadline.

Why can a circuit breaker be closed while the system is still overloaded?

The dependency may return successful but slow responses, causing queue growth. Monitor latency, concurrency and admission pressure, not only error counts.

Consistency and coordination

Why is a distributed lock lease alone unsafe?

A distributed write may be acknowledged by one node while other replicas lag. Strong consistency, eventual consistency and session guarantees solve different requirements; do not apply CAP as a simplistic ranking of databases. Under a network partition, a system must define which operations remain available and which consistency invariants are preserved. Leases and distributed locks require careful expiry and fencing: an old owner paused by GC may resume after its lease expires. A monotonically increasing fencing token lets the protected resource reject stale owners.

Why is a distributed lock lease alone unsafe?

A paused holder can resume after lease expiry and write concurrently with a new owner unless the resource rejects stale fencing generations.

Saga and compensation boundaries

Why is compensation not the same as rollback?

A multi-service purchase may reserve inventory, authorize payment and schedule fulfillment. No single local transaction spans those independent services. A saga records progress and invokes compensating actions on failure, but compensation is not a true rollback: a refund may be delayed, external emails cannot be unsent, and retries can duplicate effects. Persist state transitions, use idempotent commands and events, and reconcile uncertain outcomes. Choose orchestration when centralized visibility is important, choreography when coupling and ownership make decentralized events practical.

Why is compensation not the same as rollback?

External effects may be irreversible or only approximately reversible, and compensations can fail independently.

SLO-based reliability engineering

What does an error budget change operationally?

Define a service-level indicator such as successful requests under 300 ms, and a target SLO such as 99.9% over 30 days. At 1,000,000 eligible requests, a 0.1% error budget allows 1,000 bad requests. Track burn rate to detect fast budget consumption; do not hide failures by excluding inconvenient traffic from the denominator. Correlate error budget, dependency timeouts, saturation, retries and release changes. Use load shedding to preserve critical paths when the system cannot serve all demand.

What does an error budget change operationally?

It creates a quantified trade-off between feature delivery and reliability work, supported by measurable burn-rate alerts.
