11-DISTRIBUTED-SYSTEMS · TECHNICAL CHAPTER

Timeouts, Retries and Partial Failure

Why distributed calls fail ambiguously and how to avoid retry storms and cascading outages.

Partial failure is normal Deadline propagation Retry amplification Circuit breakers and bulkheads Load shedding and recovery

1. Partial failure is normal

What does a timeout prove?

In a single process, a function call either returns or throws in a relatively constrained environment. Across a network, the caller cannot reliably distinguish a failed server from a lost response or a partition. A timeout is a local decision to stop waiting. The remote operation may still be executing or already committed.

2. Deadline propagation

Why are independent 2s timeouts dangerous?

Allocate a request-level deadline and derive smaller per-hop budgets. If a 900 ms user-facing budget is spent 700 ms upstream, a downstream call should not start a fresh 2-second wait. Propagate cancellation where possible, but never assume cancellation rolls back remote side effects. Track remaining time, not just independent timeout constants.

3. Retry amplification

Who should own retries?

If three service layers each retry a failed call three times, the leaf dependency can see up to 27 attempts for one logical request, depending on interpretation of attempt count. Centralize retries at a deliberate boundary, use capped exponential backoff with jitter, and retry only errors likely to recover. Respect Retry-After and service-level budgets.

4. Circuit breakers and bulkheads

What does a breaker actually protect?

A circuit breaker temporarily stops calls to a failing dependency, preventing wasted work while allowing probes for recovery. It does not fix the dependency. Bulkheads bound concurrency or isolate pools so one slow integration cannot exhaust all request threads. A breaker without sensible fallback or load shedding can still produce a user-visible outage.

5. Load shedding and recovery

Why reject instead of queue forever?

When arrival rate exceeds sustainable service rate, queues grow and tail latency collapses. Bound queues, reject early with 429/503 where appropriate, and make clients back off. Recovery requires avoiding synchronized retry bursts and respecting downstream capacity. Use chaos tests for packet loss, slow responses, DNS errors and region failures.

Interview and design review

Try answering before expanding.
Why is a timeout not equivalent to a failure?

The client stopped waiting, but the server may still have committed the operation. Retrying without idempotency can duplicate effects.

How can retries trigger an outage?

Many clients retry together, amplifying demand on an already saturated dependency. Use jitter, caps, budgets and admission control.

When should you avoid retries?

For permanent validation errors, operations without a safe deduplication contract, or when the deadline/retry budget is exhausted.

What is the difference between bulkhead and circuit breaker?

A bulkhead limits resource consumption by an integration. A circuit breaker temporarily stops attempts based on observed failures. They address different failure propagation paths.

Retry-budget arithmetic

Why can retries delay recovery?

Assume each service layer allows 3 total attempts, including the first. Across three independently retrying layers, one original request may generate up to 3×3×3=27 leaf calls. If 500 original requests per second hit a degraded dependency, the worst-case attempt rate could reach 13,500 per second before backoff and other limits. This is why retries must be budgeted end to end. Prefer one intentional retry owner, cap attempts and elapsed time, apply full jitter, and honor the remaining request deadline. Couple retries with load shedding and an idempotency contract. Do not retry a timed-out payment POST unless the logical operation can be deduplicated.

Why can retries delay recovery?

Retries multiply demand precisely when a dependency has reduced capacity, increasing queueing and further timeouts.

Deadline and retry propagation

Trace the invariant, failure boundary and recovery.

A request has 800 ms total budget. After 250 ms in authentication and 150 ms in a database call, only 400 ms remain before accounting for serialization and network overhead. A downstream timeout of 1 second is therefore meaningless: it outlives the caller’s deadline. Propagate a deadline or remaining budget, reserve time for cleanup, and cancel work where possible. Retries must fit inside that budget and should be owned by one layer to avoid multiplicative amplification. If three layers each allow three attempts, one user request can produce up to 27 leaf attempts. Circuit breakers protect against repeated calls to a failing dependency, while bulkheads bound resource consumption; neither replaces admission control or an idempotency contract.

Design review and interview follow-ups

Should a timed-out POST be retried automatically?

Only if the operation has an appropriate idempotency or reconciliation contract and the retry fits within the remaining deadline.

Why can a circuit breaker be closed while the system is still overloaded?

The dependency may return successful but slow responses, causing queue growth. Monitor latency, concurrency and admission pressure, not only error counts.

Consistency and coordination

Why is a distributed lock lease alone unsafe?

A distributed write may be acknowledged by one node while other replicas lag. Strong consistency, eventual consistency and session guarantees solve different requirements; do not apply CAP as a simplistic ranking of databases. Under a network partition, a system must define which operations remain available and which consistency invariants are preserved. Leases and distributed locks require careful expiry and fencing: an old owner paused by GC may resume after its lease expires. A monotonically increasing fencing token lets the protected resource reject stale owners.

Why is a distributed lock lease alone unsafe?

A paused holder can resume after lease expiry and write concurrently with a new owner unless the resource rejects stale fencing generations.

Saga and compensation boundaries

Why is compensation not the same as rollback?

A multi-service purchase may reserve inventory, authorize payment and schedule fulfillment. No single local transaction spans those independent services. A saga records progress and invokes compensating actions on failure, but compensation is not a true rollback: a refund may be delayed, external emails cannot be unsent, and retries can duplicate effects. Persist state transitions, use idempotent commands and events, and reconcile uncertain outcomes. Choose orchestration when centralized visibility is important, choreography when coupling and ownership make decentralized events practical.

Why is compensation not the same as rollback?

External effects may be irreversible or only approximately reversible, and compensations can fail independently.

SLO-based reliability engineering

What does an error budget change operationally?

Define a service-level indicator such as successful requests under 300 ms, and a target SLO such as 99.9% over 30 days. At 1,000,000 eligible requests, a 0.1% error budget allows 1,000 bad requests. Track burn rate to detect fast budget consumption; do not hide failures by excluding inconvenient traffic from the denominator. Correlate error budget, dependency timeouts, saturation, retries and release changes. Use load shedding to preserve critical paths when the system cannot serve all demand.

What does an error budget change operationally?

It creates a quantified trade-off between feature delivery and reliability work, supported by measurable burn-rate alerts.

Distributed failure, deadline and saga lab

Time budgets are end-to-end

A client gives 900 ms. Authentication takes 120 ms, request routing and validation 80 ms, and a database read 260 ms; about 440 ms remain before serialization and network reserve. A downstream timeout of 1,000 ms cannot be useful because it exceeds the remaining request budget. Pass an absolute deadline or remaining budget, reserve time for cleanup and avoid starting work that has no realistic chance of completing.

Retry multiplication

Client: 3 attempts
    API gateway: 3 attempts each
        Order service: 3 attempts each
            Payment dependency: up to 27 attempts per user operation

Not every error is retryable. Decide which layer owns retries and propagate deadlines. Apply exponential backoff with jitter only if there is time left. A retry budget such as a small fraction of normal requests caps amplification. Idempotency or reconciliation is necessary before retrying side effects.

Circuit breakers, bulkheads and admission control

A circuit breaker reduces calls to a dependency known to be failing, but it cannot detect every capacity problem. A bulkhead separates concurrency budgets so one dependency does not consume every worker. Admission control rejects excess work before queues grow without bound. Use all three as complementary tools, and instrument pending work and queue wait, not just HTTP errors.

Saga workflow

Order created -> inventory reserved -> payment authorized -> shipment scheduled
                      |                     |
                  failure -> release    failure -> void/refund

A saga is a sequence of local transactions with compensating operations. Compensation is not a database rollback: an email cannot be unsent, refunds are asynchronous, and a provider may be temporarily unavailable. Each forward step and compensation needs an idempotent identity and durable state. Use explicit states such as PENDING, RESERVED, AUTHORIZED, COMPENSATING, COMPLETED and MANUAL_REVIEW, with transitions constrained by expected prior state.

Leases and fencing

A node holds a distributed lock lease, pauses for GC, and wakes up after another node acquired the expired lock. Both may believe they own the work. A monotonically increasing fencing token, validated by the protected resource, stops the old holder from modifying shared state. If the resource cannot validate tokens, the lock by itself does not create a strong correctness boundary.

SLO and error budget

An SLO of 99.9% successful requests across 1 million eligible requests permits 1,000 failures in the measured window. Define eligible requests, treatment of client cancellations and the latency criterion before calculating. Error-budget burn rate matters more than a single day's percentage. A high burn rate during release can justify stopping deployment and reducing risk.

Interview: answered

Why is a timeout not proof the operation failed? The remote side may commit before the response is lost. Reconcile ambiguous outcomes.

What is the difference between timeout and deadline? Timeout bounds a local wait; deadline fixes the end-to-end latest acceptable completion time.

How can a breaker show healthy while the system is melting? Calls may succeed slowly, consuming concurrency; monitor latency, saturation and queues.

Can a saga deliver atomic transactions across services? No. It provides eventual workflow coordination and compensation, with intermediate states exposed by design.

What is the failure mode of an expiring distributed lock? Stale owners may resume after lease expiry and overwrite new work without fencing.

ANSWERED ADVANCED INTERVIEW BANK · 12 SCENARIOS

Distributed reliability: Senior Interview and Design Review

Answer before reading. Every question pairs a technical explanation with a verification exercise or architectural follow-up.

1. What is a deadline and how does it differ from timeout?

Explain the trade-off and test.

A timeout bounds one local wait; an end-to-end deadline is the latest acceptable completion for the operation. Downstream calls must use the remaining budget minus overhead and cleanup reserve. Independent full timeouts at each hop can outlast client interest and waste capacity.

Follow-up / exercise: Budget 900 ms across authentication, DB and payment service calls.

2. Why can retries multiply exponentially?

Explain the trade-off and test.

When each of several layers retries independently, leaf attempts are a product of each layer's attempt count. Three attempts at each of three levels can produce 27 leaf calls for one user request. Assign retry ownership, use jitter and budgets, propagate deadlines and ensure safe operation identity.

Follow-up / exercise: Simulate transient 503 responses during a database incident.

3. What is a circuit breaker good for?

Explain the trade-off and test.

A breaker temporarily limits calls to a dependency suspected to be failing, allowing recovery and protecting some resources. It is not a substitute for concurrency limits, queue control or deadlines, and may not open if slow successes cause overload. Choose a meaningful failure window and half-open trial policy.

Follow-up / exercise: Why might an API still exhaust threads with an apparently closed breaker?

4. What does a bulkhead isolate?

Explain the trade-off and test.

Separate concurrency or resource pools stop one dependency's slowdown from consuming all resources needed by others. Isolation can be by semaphore, queue, thread pool or workload priority. Too many pools waste capacity; too little isolation spreads failure.

Follow-up / exercise: Design checkout so recommendation service failure does not starve payment processing.

5. How do distributed leases fail?

Explain the trade-off and test.

A holder can pause beyond lease expiry; another owner acquires the lease, and the old holder resumes believing it still owns the resource. A monotonic fencing token checked at the resource boundary can reject stale owners. Timeouts and heartbeat success alone cannot establish exclusive state updates.

Follow-up / exercise: Construct an example involving a long JVM GC pause.

6. What is the difference between saga and distributed transaction?

Explain the trade-off and test.

A saga coordinates local commits and compensations across services with observable intermediate states. A global atomic transaction attempts a shared commit decision across participants, with coordination and availability costs. Sagas require durable workflow state, idempotent steps and manual reconciliation paths; compensation is not necessarily reversal.

Follow-up / exercise: Compare authorizing a payment and later issuing a refund.

7. Why can compensation itself fail?

Explain the trade-off and test.

An external refund can time out, inventory release can race with another reservation, and a cancellation notification cannot undo an email already delivered. Compensation steps therefore need retries, identities, time limits, visibility and a manual-review terminal state.

Follow-up / exercise: Design a saga that survives a failed refund provider.

8. What does eventual consistency require from a product?

Explain the trade-off and test.

The product must define which stale reads are acceptable, how users see pending states, when reconciliation occurs and which invariants are never violated. A vague eventual-consistency label does not answer what happens when an order shows PAID before inventory reserves.

Follow-up / exercise: Set a user-visible freshness and correction policy.

9. What does CAP actually address?

Explain the trade-off and test.

CAP frames the trade-off under a network partition between linearizable consistency and availability of operations in the formal model. It does not rank systems as simply two-of-three independent knobs, and it does not substitute for latency, durability or session-consistency analysis.

Follow-up / exercise: Describe behavior of a write when quorum cannot be reached.

10. What is an SLI versus SLO versus SLA?

Explain the trade-off and test.

An SLI is a measurement such as fraction of valid API requests completing successfully under a threshold. An SLO is the desired target over a window. An SLA is a contractual promise with terms and possible remedies. Define denominators, exclusions and aggregation before computing an error budget.

Follow-up / exercise: Calculate allowed failures for 99.9% of one million eligible requests.

11. How does load shedding improve reliability?

Explain the trade-off and test.

When service capacity is fixed, early rejection of low-priority or excess requests can protect critical work and avoid creating long timeout queues. It converts uncontrolled latent failure into a bounded, explicit overload contract. Caller retries must not immediately defeat the limit.

Follow-up / exercise: Plan graceful degradation during an inventory dependency slowdown.

12. How do you test distributed failure modes?

Explain the trade-off and test.

Inject network delays, dropped responses, duplicate messages, process crashes at persistence boundaries, partition loss and clock skew where relevant. Assert domain invariants and recovery time, not merely that endpoints returned a status. Use trace correlation and state reconciliation to verify outcomes.

Follow-up / exercise: What would falsify the claim that one logical order is never charged twice?
