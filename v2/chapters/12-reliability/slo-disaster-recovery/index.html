1. SLIs and user-visible reliability

Reliability is not simply uptime. Define an SLI from a user-relevant eligible population, such as successful checkout calls below 500 ms, over a stated window. The SLO is a target (for example 99.9%) and the error budget permits exceptions: across 1,000,000 eligible events, 0.1% is 1,000 bad events. Beware low-volume endpoints and ambiguous exclusions. Availability and durability are different properties: a service can be available while silently losing accepted writes.

2. Error budgets and multi-window burn rates

A single monthly error rate hides rapid incidents. Burn rate measures how fast the budget is consumed relative to the target rate. For a 0.1% error budget, a sustained 1% bad-event rate consumes budget at 10× the allowable pace. Short windows catch severe incidents; longer windows reduce alert noise. Tie paging decisions to user impact and enforce release risk controls during sustained budget exhaustion.

3. Failure domains and redundancy

A replica on the same host or zone does not remove host- or zone-level common failure. Enumerate process, node, zone, region, account, identity provider, DNS and control-plane dependencies. Redundancy only helps if dependencies and failover protocols are truly independent. Multi-region active-active can increase availability but adds data ownership and conflict costs; active-passive simplifies single-writer semantics but has recovery lag.

Regional control planes and dependencies can fail across every zone, and a region-wide event remains in the failure domain.

4. RPO/RTO and backup strategy

Recovery Point Objective states maximum tolerable data loss; Recovery Time Objective states target restoration duration. Backups must be restorable, encrypted, separately permissioned, and tested against production-like data volumes. A replica is not a backup: accidental deletion and corruption propagate. Ensure database snapshots are transactionally consistent or paired with logs that enable recovery to a coherent point.

5. Circuit breakers, bulkheads and load shedding

Circuit breakers suppress some calls to unhealthy dependencies; bulkheads isolate resource budgets; load shedding rejects work that would overwhelm capacity. These complement bounded queues, deadlines and priority traffic classes. Failure masking is dangerous: returning a cached success without disclosing stale state can violate business contracts. Fault inject dependency slowdown, not merely hard failure, to test saturation.

6. Recovery strategy: database and application cutover

Document a finite-state failover procedure: detect, declare, fence old writers, promote intended writer, redirect traffic, validate data consistency, watch metrics, and formally end recovery. Avoid split-brain: two writers accepting writes against divergent replicas can create conflicts. Failback is another planned migration, not a DNS flip, once writes have accrued on the promoted side.

Why must a failback plan include data reconciliation?

The old primary lacks writes accepted after promotion unless a proven replication/reconciliation path catches it up.

7. Chaos engineering and controlled game days

Choose steady-state user SLIs and a bounded blast radius first. Inject one fault at a time: pod restart, zone isolation, database election, cache loss, high latency or dropped responses. Record expected and observed behavior, rollback triggers and concrete follow-up engineering actions. Chaos experiments without guardrails or actionable hypotheses can be operationally irresponsible.

8. Operational incident response

An incident runbook must name on-call ownership, severity, containment actions, safe rollback, evidence capture, customer communication and after-action review. Prefer mitigation before exhaustive root-cause debugging when customer harm is ongoing. Separate contributing factors from a simplistic human-error explanation, and track follow-ups through verification rather than tickets alone.

5. Why aren't replicas backups?

6. How does RPO differ from RTO?

RPO limits acceptable lost data; RTO limits recovery duration.
